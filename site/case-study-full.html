Case study: separating a shared digital history with AI-assisted organization

This is an anonymized account of my experience with five drives, Google Photos, and a Google Takeout export. It is not a copy of anyone’s files. I have removed or rounded names, paths, counts, dates, places, account details, private events, and other identifying information. I do not identify the other person involved.

1. The human problem

The project began after a breakup. The photo library held years of shared life. It contained ordinary days, important moments, work, travel, and parts of a relationship that could not be reduced to neat folders.

Separating one person’s memories from another’s felt nearly impossible. Some photos clearly belonged to one person. Others mattered to both people. Some had personal meaning that was not visible in the image or its filename.

This was not only a technical problem. It was emotional. Every decision about a photo could feel like a decision about the past.

2. Why the export felt inadequate

Google Takeout provided useful files, but it did not provide a clear, usable organization for the separation. The export had folders, copies, edits, dates, and relationships that were difficult to understand together.

The files were present, but their history was not easy to follow. It was possible to see that material had been preserved without feeling that it had been made usable. The export created a new problem: how to understand a large set of files whose meaning had been spread across a service, folders, metadata, and memory.

3. When the question changed

At first, I asked a simple question: “How do I organize these photos?” That question made the work sound like a filing task. It suggested that the answer was a better folder tree.

The situation demanded a different question:

How can people use AI to make a difficult digital separation safer, clearer, and more humane?

The goal was not to erase the past or decide whose memories mattered more. The goal was to help each person recover, keep, and understand the parts of the collection that belonged to them, while preserving the shared history with care.

4. The five-drive discovery problem

The photo library was only part of the collection. Material was also spread across five physical drives. The drives held overlapping photos, project files, exports, working files, and backups.

The same event could appear as an original photo, an edited version, a smaller export, and a backup. A file could have a useful date but an unhelpful name. A folder could suggest a project without proving what the file was. It was not safe to assume that the newest copy was the right copy, or that two similar photos were duplicates.

The first practical step was to treat each drive and the Google Photos material as a separate source. I recorded paths, file types, sizes, dates, and checksums when available. Items that could not be read or explained became questions to review rather than items to hide.

5. The method that grew from the situation

The method began with discovery and delayed intervention. I kept the original sources in place and worked from inventories, copies, and staged results.

I separated facts from guesses. A matching checksum was evidence that two files had the same bytes at the time of checking. A folder name was a clue. An idea about who a photo belonged to needed human review.

I also used separate descriptions instead of forcing everything into one folder tree. A file could have a source, a project, a subject, a file type, a relationship to another copy, and a confidence level. That made it possible to say “I do not know yet” without losing the item.

Changes were copy-first and reversible where possible. The source set was preserved. Deletion was not automatic and was not part of the first pass. Proposals were reviewed by a person before any important change. Results were checked by comparing expected groups, opening sample files and albums, and confirming that originals remained available.

6. What AI helped with

AI became useful when the file list was too large and confusing to hold in mind. I used it to turn the inventory into questions, suggest neutral names for groups, summarize folder patterns, and point out possible relationships for review.

The useful result was not an answer that ended the work. It was a shorter path to the next good question. For example, a suggestion could show that several files deserved comparison, while leaving the decision open.

The person reviewing the material remained responsible for the meaning of the collection. AI suggestions were kept beside the evidence that supported or challenged them.

7. What AI could not decide

AI could not decide who owned a memory, what rights applied, which copy had the greatest personal value, or whether a file should be deleted. It could not settle an emotional question by producing a confident label.

It also could not be trusted to treat similar images as interchangeable. An edited photo may carry a different history from its original. A smaller export may be the copy someone actually shared. A duplicate may still matter because of its source, notes, or place in the story.

Important choices required human approval. The process did not use AI to approve deletion, overwrite, merging, publication, or other irreversible actions.

8. What the experience taught

The work taught me that digital organization is often a history problem, not only a naming problem. People need to know where a file came from and what happened to it before deciding where it belongs.

It also showed why a cloud library can be useful as an access layer without being the only preservation copy. A service may make photos easier to view while leaving questions about export behavior, metadata, edits, and relationships.

Most importantly, the method had to respect the people behind the files. A careful workflow can reduce confusion, but it cannot remove the human responsibility. The right goal is not a perfect archive. It is a clearer, safer, more respectful set of choices.

9. What remains uncertain

Some relationships between files remained uncertain. Some decisions depended on memory or meaning that could not be recovered from metadata. The long-term behavior of a cloud export and the future usefulness of its metadata also require continued review.

This experience did not measure AI accuracy. It did not show that one method works for every family, breakup, photo library, or set of drives. It is one person’s experience, not proof that the method works for every collection. More case studies should include mistakes, failed moves, disagreement, and situations where this method was not a good fit.
